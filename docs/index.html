    How it works

    From a jittery number to a forecast.

    The chart is real: two Boeing 737-800s at a similar level in the same half hour. One flew through rough air. Scroll, and the steps beside it walk through what Horus does.

    Vertical rate, then the per-minute index

    ft/min as broadcast · index = roughness ÷ the type's smooth-air scale

    Read the broadcast

    Vertical rate, altitude and position from public ADS-B. Rounded to 64 ft/min, and the archive only stores changes of 200 ft/min or more.

    Keep level flight

    Climbs and descents are cut out. Over 120 s windows: above FL200, within 300 ft, median rate under 200 ft/min. The shaded minutes on the chart are the level ones Horus scores.

    Measure what's left

    Remove the slow trend, correct the rounding noise (Sheppard), and divide by what this aircraft type shows in smooth air. That gives one index bar per minute.

    Alarm, forecast, check

    An index at or above the threshold is a detection. It lights its ~45 km cell; the odds fade with a fitted half-life and drift with GFS winds. Pilot reports score it.

    Try it: drag the threshold line on the chart up or down.

    Same type, smooth air

    Another 737-800, a little higher, in the same half hour. Same code, same threshold: its index never comes close, so Horus stays quiet. That is what dividing by the type's smooth-air scale buys.

    The architecture

    A sensor network made of airliners.

    ADS-B archive3.5 GB a day
    Hold and gate±10,000 ft/min
    Level flight120 s windows
    Residual σ64 ft/min fixed
    Per-type index≥ 1.0× alarms
    Forecast grid8,388 cells
    CheckPIREPs score it

    One day of public ADS-B, streamed.

    Each archive day, about 3.5 GB, is streamed and never written to disk. Only continental-US traffic at FL180 and above is kept, about 220 MB, as typed, unit-checked Parquet. make up fetches what is missing.

    Rebuild the signal, drop the impossible.

    The archive stores a new vertical rate only when it moves 200 ft/min, so Horus rebuilds a held one-second series, and gates out values beyond ±10,000 ft/min, such as the field-maximum glitch code.

    Keep level flight.

    Climbs and descents are cut: 120 s windows above FL200, within 300 ft, median rate under 200 ft/min.

    Measure the jitter.

    Remove the slow trend, correct ADS-B's 64 ft/min rounding (Sheppard), and what is left is the roughness, in ft/min.

    Put every type on one scale.

    Divide by what that aircraft type shows in smooth air, so a business jet and a widebody compare. At 1.0× and above, it is a detection.

    Light the grid.

    Each detection raises the odds in its ~45 km cell, one of 8,388 cells × 13 levels. The odds fade with a fitted half-life (50.3 min at cruise) and drift with GFS winds.

    Check against pilots.

    Pilot reports never go in. They only score Horus, on January days the models never saw: detector PR-AUC 0.566 against 0.290 for random scores.

    One core, offline and live.

    The same pure functions run the benchmark and the live loop, and a test requires bit-identical results. Time-split and leakage-tested, 32.3 ms per grid update for 10,000 aircraft, built with uv, no Docker.

    Detector

    Ranking 1,788 pilot-reported turbulence windows against 4,388 quiet ones. Random scores get 0.290; each method adds one idea, and B3, the per-type index, is what Horus runs.

    Forecast

    How well the grid ranks where the next detection will be, 15 minutes ahead. Climatology scores 0.500; moving the odds with the wind (N2) is the step that works, and negative evidence (N3) made it worse.

    Fading

    One detection keeps predicting the next aircraft's for about 50.3 minutes at cruise, and 11.6 minutes lower down, fitted on December data by altitude band.

    Speed

    From a snapshot arriving to the updated map takes 2.9 s at p95 for about 2,462 aircraft, well inside the 10 s budget; a full grid update takes 32.3 ms.

    The honest part

    At a strict false-alarm setting, the engine isolates high-confidence turbulence but currently captures a low overall percentage of reported events.

    0.2%of reported turbulence caught at 1 false alarm per 1,000 flight-hours
    5.7%at 10 false alarms per 1,000 flight-hours

    Why: most turbulence never gets a pilot report, so every "false alarm" may be real but unreported, and the public archive drops the small jitter that light chop leaves in the data.

    Weak labels
    Pilot reports are sparse, late and roughly placed, and most turbulence is never reported, so false-alarm rates are upper bounds.
    Coarse public data
    The archive stores a new vertical rate only when it moves 200 ft/min or more (plus a ~20 s heartbeat). Smaller jitter is lost.
    Replay, not live
    Free live feeds give about one point per aircraft every 90 s, too sparse to detect, so the live loop runs on an archived day.
    One winter, one region
    44 days of data, continental US, FL200 to FL460.
    Roughness, not EDR
    Measured in ft/min per aircraft type; not calibrated to eddy dissipation rate, the aviation standard.
    Negative evidence hurt
    Lowering the odds after smooth passes made the forecast worse (ROC-AUC 0.471), so it stays off.

    Run it

    Three commands.

    macOS or Linux with uv. Public data only, no account or API key. The first make up streams about 3.5 GB.

    $ make up      # environment + the demo day's data
$ make watch   # live text stream: an archived afternoon at 30x
$ make cases   # three case studies, minute by minute
